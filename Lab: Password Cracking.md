## Associated Research
What makes a good password policy? Password policy is critically important to the safety of an organization, because, many times, it is the main wall between outsiders and access to a vital account. By many it is considered “the first line of defense in protecting network systems.” (Guo and Zhang 2018) Of all cybercrime losses, “every third data breach can be linked directly to password management.” (Glöckler et al. 2024) Given its importance, it is paramount to put adequate emphasis on the secure creation and management of passwords. People tend to form most of their passwords along patterns which have something to do with them, personally. Based on these patterns, simply knowing something about a person can give an attacker an advantage in guessing their victim’s password. In fact, “These patterns typically follow one of several common rules: dictionary words, numeric sequences (like phone numbers or birthdates), or symbolic masks—with symbols rarely used alone.” (Rzayeva Leila et al. 2025) There are many ways to combat this issue, such as length. Password length is a great option, because it creates more content to crack. However, “simple policy based on longer passwords, despite its usability and some security advantages, may not be quite suitable for real-world deployment.” (Shay et al. 2016) There needs to be more complexity to a password than just length. Another option is requiring special characters, which increases the number of possible passwords and increases security. Many organizations see the need to enforce strong password policy, so they establish requirements for their users and employees. However, there is a balance between how easy a password is to remember and how strong it is. It becomes harder to prevent users and employees from storing their passwords in public areas when policy is too strict.

I’d like to discuss school password policy here at UT San Antonio. First, I’d like to say that however strong or weak the password policy itself may be for student users like me, I’m especially impressed and assured by the fact that there is consistent multifactor authentication used for each application for my account. Whether I’m looking to log into my account to check my homepage, opening my email to view notifications, or looking for sources on the online library page, I’m briefly asked to use DUO to verify my login request. This gives me peace of mind knowing that any unknown device logging into my account would trigger a DUO notification, asking if I was trying to log in. For the password policy itself, there are few requirements that will disallow account creation if not met. I remember from when I created my account that, to create a new account, you must create a password that is at least 15 characters long. However, I found the passwords/passphrases FAQ page that UT San Antonio released which talks about other requirements for password creation. It mentions that the standard password must be at least 15 characters long, but there is no restriction on types and characters. It then requires that passwords must not contain common phrases or patterns with numbers or letters. It also cannot be the same as a previous password or be guessable by knowing personal information. This content can be found at the following UT San Antonio FAQ website: https://passphrase.utsa.edu/FAQ. There are other recommendations that follow, but none are required. So far, I have not had any login attempts that were not from me, because DUO has not notified me of any attempts. I use other passwords for school and keep them in a separate device in a file that can’t be accessed from my workstation. I also have multifactor authentication set up on several of my accounts. Some of my passwords are stored in Google Password Manager where my PIN is required to access any passwords. I also appreciate that any service that accepts my UT San Antonio login for free access takes me to the login portal, which enforces the same password policy on each software. For work, the only password policy I live under is the one I enforce. I make it a practice to keep my passwords and passphrases complex and stored securely to avoid unauthorized access. 

How are passwords related to Identity and Access Management (IAM)? To put it simply, password management is one of the services that identity and access management companies provide to their clients. There are, of course, other services beyond just password management. The exact services that an IAM system provides depends on the company. In fact, they may provide “enterprises with tools to support them in handling and monitoring a growing number of identities beyond employees, such as external partners, customers, and – driven by the growing  relevance of the Internet of Things (IOT) – smart devices.” (Glöckler et al. 2024) Services such as AWS provide many options for clients, which makes them a great overall business tool to protect companies from outside threats. However, password management by itself is crucial to the services IAM gives. What is IAM? It involves the “management of identities as well as the processes associated with authentication and authorization processes in organizations.” (Glöckler et al. 2024) Authentication and authorization are two different processes. Authorization is extremely important as it pertains to the process of allowing or restricting access to assets based on a set of rules. Without an IAM system, many employees accumulate too much privilege from “ineffective and application-specific user management.” (Hummer et al. 2016) This violates the best practice of enforcing least privilege. However, authentication is the topic I want to look at, because it is the process of verifying the identity of an individual who is accessing an account, which is where passwords are involved. This is the first wall a user or attacker would face. Before an account is even compared to permission rules for authorization, an account must first be verified. Different accounts may need different levels of authentication. IAM services manage the customizable sets of rules around passwords in an organization. For example, if an organization that uses an IAM service wants to increase password restrictions for a specific group, they could access their dashboard and tailor which rules they want for that specific group. This is very valuable for organizations, because they need to manage permissions for potentially hundreds of employees with different roles and responsibilities. Some employees may need to be held to higher password policy standards due to their responsibilities. One responsibility that comes with the use of an IAM system is regular manual updates. While this tools saves time, it is still important for there to be regular updates to passwords and other system settings to avoid becoming dated. Overall, IAM is a great tool to fill needs for a flexible growing company, and password management is one of the fundamental premises it is built on.
